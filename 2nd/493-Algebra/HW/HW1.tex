Let $H$ be a subgroup of $D_4$ by Lagrange's Theorem the order of $H$ can only be $1,2,4,8$

Case 1: \(H\) has order 1
\begin{itemize}
    \item The only subgroup of order 1 is the trivial subgroup \( \{e\} \).
    \item Since \( \{e\} \) is already on our list, \( H \) cannot be of order 1 unless it is \( \{e\} \), which is already accounted for.
\end{itemize}

Case 2: \(H\) has order 2
\begin{itemize}
    \item If \( H \) has order 2, it must consist of \( e \) and one other element \( g \), where \( g^2 = e \).
    \item In \( D_4 \), the elements that satisfy \( g^2 = e \) are \( x^2 \), \( y \), \( yx \), \( yx^2 \), and \( yx^3 \).
    \item Each of these elements generates a subgroup of order 2:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \( \langle x^2 \rangle = \{ e, x^2 \} \)
        \item \( \langle y \rangle = \{ e, y \} \)
        \item \( \langle yx \rangle = \{ e, yx \} \)
        \item \( \langle yx^2 \rangle = \{ e, yx^2 \} \)
        \item \( \langle yx^3 \rangle = \{ e, yx^3 \} \)
    \end{itemize}
    \item Therefore, any subgroup \( H \) of order 2 must be one of these, and all are already on our list.
\end{itemize}

Case 3: \(H\) has order 4
\begin{itemize}
    \item If $H$ has an element of order $4$ this implies $x \in H$ or $x^3 \in H$ since $\ang{x}=\ang{x^3}$ this implies $\ang{x} \subset H$ but since they both have 4 elements this means $H=\ang{x}$
    \item If $H$ does not have an element of order $4$ this implies all non-identity elements must be order 2 by Lagrange's Theorem. The elements
    of order 2 are $\{x^2,y,yx,yx^2,yx^3\}$
    \begin{itemize}
        \item If $yx^3 \in H$ and we then add $a \in \{x^2,y,yx,yx^2\}$
        \begin{itemize}
            \item If $a=x^2$ then $yx^3x^2=x^2xy=yx \in H \implies H=\ang{x^2,yx}$
            \item If $a=yx$ then $yx^3yx=x^2 \in H \implies H=\ang{x^2,yx}$
            \item If $a=y$ then $yx^3y=x^3 \in H \implies$ is bigger than 4.
            \item If $a=yx^2$ then $yx^3yx^2=x \in H \implies$ is bigger than 4.
        \end{itemize}

        \item If $yx \in H$ and we then add $a \in \{x^2,y,yx^2,yx^3\}$
        \begin{itemize}
            \item If $a=x^2$ then $yxx^2=yx^3 \in H \implies H=\ang{x^2,yx}$
            \item If $a=yx^3$ then $yx^3yx=x^2 \in H \implies H=\ang{x^2,yx}$
            \item If $a=y$ then $yyx=x \in H \implies$ is bigger than 4.
            \item If $a=yx^2$ then $yx^2yx=x^3 \in H \implies$ is bigger than 4.
        \end{itemize}

        \item If $y \in H$ and we then add $a \in \{x^2,yx,yx^2,yx^3\}$
        \begin{itemize}
            \item If $a=x^2$ then $yx^2 \in H \implies H=\ang{x^2,y}$
            \item If $a=yx^2$ then $x^2 \in H \implies H=\ang{x^2,y}$
            \item If $a=yx^3$ then $yyx^3=x^3 \in H \implies$ is bigger than 4.
            \item If $a=yx$ then $yyx=x \in H \implies$ is bigger than 4.
        \end{itemize}

        \item If $x^2 \in H$ and we then add $a \in \{y,yx,yx^2,yx^3\}$
        \begin{itemize}
            \item If $a=y$ then $yx^2 \in H \implies H=\ang{x^2,y}$
            \item If $a=yx$ then $yx^3 \in H \implies H=\ang{x^2,yx}$
            \item If $a=yx^2$ then $y \in H \implies H=\ang{x^2,y}$
            \item If $a=yx^3$ then $yx \in H \implies H=\ang{x^2,yx}$
        \end{itemize}
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

Case 4: \(H\) has order 8
\begin{itemize}
    \item If \( H \) has order 8, it must be the whole group \( D_4 \).
    \item Therefore, \( H \) would be \( D_4 \), which is already on our list.
\end{itemize}
\phantom{text}\\
In each case, we’ve shown that any possible subgroup \( H \) of \( D_4 \) must be one of the subgroups already listed. Therefore, no additional subgroups can exist, and our list of subgroups is complete.

\subsection*{Problem 5}

   \begin{proof}
     Initially, consider the set of all pairs \( (h, k) \) where \( h \in H \) and \( k \in K \). The total number of such pairs is \( |H| \times |K| \). If all such pairs \( (h, k) \) produced distinct elements in \( HK \), then \( |HK| = |H| \times |K| \). However, since \( H \) and \( K \) may have non-trivial intersection, some products \( hk \) may coincide, leading to overcounting.\\
     Suppose \( h_a k_a = h_b k_b \) for some \( h_a, h_b \in H \) and \( k_a, k_b \in K \). This implies:
     \[
     h_a^{-1} h_b = k_a k_b^{-1}.
     \]
     The left-hand side, \( h_a^{-1} h_b \), is an element of \( H \), and the right-hand side, \( k_a k_b^{-1} \), is an element of \( K \). Thus, \( h_a^{-1} h_b \) must be an element of \( H \cap K \), meaning:
     \[
     h_a^{-1} h_b \in H \cap K \quad \text{and} \quad k_a k_b^{-1} \in H \cap K.
     \]
     Therefore, if \( h_a k_a = h_b k_b \), then the pairs \( (h_a, k_a) \) and \( (h_b, k_b) \) correspond to the same element in \( HK \), and the number of distinct elements in \( HK \) is reduced by a factor equal to the size of the intersection \( H \cap K \).\\
     Therefore, the number of distinct elements in \( HK \) is exactly \( \frac{|H| \cdot |K|}{|H \cap K|} \), establishing that
     \[
     |HK| = \frac{|H| \cdot |K|}{|H \cap K|}.
     \]
   \end{proof}
